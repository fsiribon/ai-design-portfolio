Week 08 · Hallucination · Week 1 Proposal

01 / Introduce the game

THE WRONG DOOR

Every door looks possible. Not every door should be opened.

A short browser-based psychological puzzle set in an ordinary hallway at night. The player is trying to get home, using one simple action: click a door. What begins as a readable space becomes a test of whether visible clues can still be trusted.

  • Short browser game
  • One main interaction
  • Opening + 3 stages + ending
  • Trust → notice → doubt → reconsider

02 / Present the case study

The same action can change its meaning.

I played Geometric Somnium, a browser game by Chezz Studios. Its visual language is not the model for my game. The useful lesson is behavioral: I began by chasing everything and ended by deciding what was worth approaching.

Creator statement · verified primary source

What the official page confirms

  • Players interact with triangles, squares, keys, and other shapes to gain lucidity.
  • Reaching 100% lucidity causes the player to awaken and the game to restart.
  • Keys add 25 lucidity.
  • Other shapes can darken the screen, split, interfere with controls, spawn shapes, increase lucidity, or decrease health.
  • Shapes disappear after being touched, and each playthrough generates a random set of colored shapes.
  • The creator discloses that AI assisted with some of the code.

The source does not establish a universal color-to-effect rule. Color-specific effects are therefore not presented here as creator-defined mechanics.

My observation

Three meaningful moments

Actual screenshots from my playthrough, paired with what changed in my understanding.

Moment 01

I thought everything was collectible

At first, every colorful shape looked like something I should catch. The same basic action soon produced very different consequences.

Interpretation: automatic collection became uncertainty.

Moment 02

Learning to choose

After helpful and harmful interactions, I stopped chasing every visible object. I began observing first and deciding whether it was worth approaching.

Interpretation: progress involved selective attention.

Moment 03

Awakening

At 100% lucidity, the screen displayed “AWAKENING” and explained that maximum lucidity had been reached before restarting.

Interpretation: the objective became completely clear only at the end.

My play

Six player-action events

Action → response → understanding

# Player action Game response Player understanding
01 I chased colorful shapes because I assumed they were collectibles. Different interactions produced different results. Not every visible object should automatically be collected.
02 I touched objects that appeared useful. Some interactions improved my progress or condition. Some shapes can help me.

03 / Critical discussion with AI

The useful insight appeared after I challenged the first answer.

The first analysis described the game mainly as trial and error. My observations showed that this was incomplete: the more important change was from automatic action to distrust and selective attention.

Gap 01 / Interaction

The analysis missed how my behavior changed.

I did not keep experimenting randomly. I first chased everything; later I watched, avoided, and chose.

Gap 02 / Evidence

A visual pattern was treated as a verified rule.

The official page confirms possible effects, but not a fixed color-to-effect code. My color association remains an observation, not a creator statement.

Me / Challenge

“You described the game mainly as trial and error, but in my first play I thought every colorful shape was something I should collect. Later I realized that some interactions could hurt me or make more shapes appear, so I started avoiding objects instead of trying everything. Does this mean the game is also about distrust and selective attention, not only trial and error?”

AI / Revision

“Yes. Trial and error explains how you discovered the rules, but not how your behavior changed. Once similar-looking objects produced different consequences, interaction became a decision about trust. The game moved you from automatic collection toward observation and selective attention.”

Me / New evidence

“The official creator page lists several possible shape effects, but it does not say that a particular color always gives a particular effect. We should separate what I observed from what the creator actually confirms.”

AI / Revised insight

“The safer interpretation is that uncertainty comes from the consequences of interaction, not from a verified universal color code. Your personal color observation can be documented as an observation, but not as a creator-defined rule.”

My conclusion

The same simple action—approaching and touching something—does not always mean the same thing. The player gradually learns not to trust every invitation to interact.

Design decision

My game uses one equally simple action: click a door. Across three stages, visual clues conflict, change, and lead into loops.

“Hallucination becomes interesting when the player cannot immediately trust what they see or what an interaction will do.”

04 / Narrative and concept

An ordinary hallway becomes unreliable.

Hallucination is represented as a gradual loss of confidence in visible evidence. The player does not simply see bizarre imagery; ordinary information stops behaving consistently.

World

A hallway at night

Repeated apartment or hotel doors, room numbers, EXIT signs, warm lights, quiet objects, and sounds behind doors. Numbers change, directions disagree, and spaces repeat.

Player role

Someone trying to get home

There is no complicated backstory. The player’s identity is defined by one immediate goal: find the door that leads home.

Core rule

Click a door

The control never changes. What changes is the player’s confidence that a label, light, sound, or direction can be believed.

Intended experience

Unresolved relief

Recognition becomes trust, trust becomes doubt, and doubt becomes reconsideration. Even the apparent escape leaves one contradiction behind.

Arrive Wonder Doubt Change Leave

06 / Style and sound guide

A familiar hallway with an unstable center.

The visual design begins warm, symmetrical, restrained, and believable. Hallucination appears through small contradictions in information—not through constant glitch effects.

Warm Amber#D6A85FFamiliarity / apparent safety
Charcoal#252321Hallway darkness
Muted Cream#D8D0C2Walls / neutral surfaces
Deep Red#7B3030Warning / doubt
Cold Blue#52667AUnreliable perception
Typography

INTER / Georgia

Inter and the existing portfolio sans-serif keep instructions legible. A restrained serif italic is reserved for reflective questions and editorial contrast.

Interface

Only what the player needs

Hallway, three doors, environmental clues, and one short instruction. No score, inventory, or complicated HUD.

Motion

Contradiction, not decoration

Stable in Stage 1; small flicker and number changes in Stage 2; slight extension and label displacement in Stage 3. Reduced-motion mode removes nonessential movement.

Sound

Silence makes the ending legible

Ventilation hum, electrical buzz, footsteps, latch, hinge, low wrong-choice tone, and soft morning ambience. Sound remains off by default and user-controlled.

07 / Two-week build plan

Build the complete loop first. Refine only after it reads clearly.

Week 2 produces a complete rough game from opening to ending. Week 3 tests whether first-time players understand the interaction and feel the intended uncertainty.

Week 2 / Playable prototype

Build

  1. Day 1: Three clickable doors, focus states, correct/incorrect logic, transition, restart, mouse/touch, keyboard.
  2. Day 2: Opening and Stage 1 with numbered doors, warm-light clue, advance, and loop.
  3. Day 3: Stage 2 with EXIT sign, conflicting clues, changing numbers, and subtle flicker.
  4. Day 4: Stage 3 with HOME / EXIT / ???, contradictory clues, and final choice.
  5. Day 5: Morning transition, “YOU FOUND HOME,” 27 → 28, final question, and restart.
  6. Weekend: Desktop, mobile, keyboard, touch, restart, sound-control, and reduced-motion self-test.

End of Week 2: a complete rough browser game with an opening, three short stages, and an ending.

Week 3 / Test + refine + publish

Test

  1. Understanding: Can players discover that clicking a door is the main interaction?
  2. Trust: When do they realize the visual clues may be unreliable?
  3. Decision: Why did they choose their final door?
  4. Ending: How do they interpret the changing room number?
  5. Refine: Clue visibility, loops, timing, sound, mobile input, keyboard access, and reduced motion.
  6. Publish: Verify the public URL, responsive layouts, controls, replay, and documented feedback.

End of Week 3: a tested, accessible, published game with intentional revisions.

Required

Opening, three doors, Stages 1–3, wrong-choice loops, final choice, ending, restart, responsive layout.

Optional

Advanced footsteps, stereo knocks, elaborate door animation, deeper distortion, alternate ending lines.

Cut first

Stereo audio, advanced distortion, extra animation, and extra endings. Never cut the complete stage sequence.

Success test

A first-time player reaches the ending, recognizes unreliable clues by Stage 2, and can explain why the final choice felt uncertain.
